Jan Walker Books · sources

Ultimate Tesla Challenge

Ninety questions, and for each of them the source that settles it. Figures and dates come from the company's own filings and from regulators wherever those exist; anything a manufacturer had reason to phrase generously is checked against an independent test. Owner forums are used to find out which question is worth asking, never to answer it.

Two retellings of one press release are one source, not two. Eight of these entries rest on a single source; each is marked one source and says in its note why a second was not found. Where the record is genuinely disputed, the book says so in the answer instead of choosing the more impressive number.

Corrections made in review

Found by re-checking every question one at a time, after a reviewer rejected a part-by-part check. Listed here rather than quietly fixed.

  • Model 3 deliveries for 2017. Was: 1,764 → now: 1,770. 1,764 is an estimate of US sales only. Tesla's own 8-K exhibits give 220 in Q3 and 1,550 in Q4.
  • Model 3 reservations. Was: more than 115,000 in the first 24 hours → now: 115,000 on the opening day, over 180,000 within 24 hours. 115,000 was the count before the car was unveiled on the evening of 31 March 2016, not the day's total.
  • The millionth Model 3. Was: the question asked about production → now: the question asks about sales. The sources count deliveries, not output; the wording now matches what can be shown.
  • Model Y as the world's best seller in 2023. Was: stated without qualification → now: a note on how the ranking is counted. The crown depends on whether every Corolla body style is totalled under one name.
  • The remark about the Model X's complexity. Was: paraphrased → now: the words themselves, with dates. A direct quotation can be checked; a paraphrase cannot.
  • Falcon-wing door clearance. Was: “less than a foot” → now: 30 cm stated. The source gives 30 cm of clear space on each side.
  • Cybertruck stainless steel. Was: stated without qualification → now: a note about the orange spots. Owners report surface staining; the book should not promise more than the source does.
  • The gap between 1.99 and 2.07 seconds. Was: explained by the rollout allowance → now: a question about what rollout is, shown on the 2.07 / 2.28 pair from one run. MotorTrend's 2.07 s already excludes rollout; without it the same run is 2.28 s. What separates 2.07 from the maker's 1.99 s is surface preparation, not where the clock starts.
  • The quarter mile, 9.34 s at 152.2 mph. Was: presented as a recorded run → now: stated as MotorTrend's estimate, worked out from the run data. No full quarter mile was run on the surface available; the figure is derived from the data rather than clocked at the end of a strip.
  • What the prepared surface is worth. Was: “about as much again” as the rollout allowance → now: about another tenth, against the rollout's fifth of a second. The figures give 0.21 s for rollout (2.28 to 2.07) and 0.09 s for surface (2.07 to 1.98). The claim stood in two places, the front matter and the answer.
  • The timeline entry for 29 September 2015. Was: “Model X unveiled” → now: “First Model X deliveries”. The question itself had been fixed but the back-matter timeline still carried the old wording; the prototype was shown in February 2012.
  • The tug-of-war. Was: “weight alone was enough to decide it” → now: load on the driven wheels buys grip, with tyres and surface named as the other terms. Weight alone cannot be said to decide the result without accounting for tyres and surface.
  • The single large casting on the Model 3. Was: credited to the 2017 Model 3 → now: said to have come later, and first on the crossover. The one-piece rear underbody reached volume production on the Model Y in early 2021.
